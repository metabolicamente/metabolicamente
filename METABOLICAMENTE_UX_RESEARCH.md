# Metabolicamente — UX & Product Research Report
*Deep research, verified against sources. July 11, 2026. For the English demo (pre-August) and November MVP.*

## Executive summary — the ten decisions this research supports

1. Call the scheduling object an **appointment**, the clinical encounter a **session**, and the people you serve **patients** (addressed as "you/your" in the UI).
2. Keep the **dark green + cream** palette — it's the proven pattern for warm-but-clinical health brands (One Medical, Parsley Health, Maven, Nourish) — but differentiate with typography and one warm accent, and never signal patient status with color alone.
3. Doctor/nutritionist dashboards should show a **small, targeted set of indicators**, not everything: trend-over-time is the single most valuable element, and clinicians actively dislike cluttered multi-line charts.
4. Flag patients with a **passive, non-interruptive status column** (defined red/amber/green thresholds), not pop-up alerts — clinicians override 49–96% of interruptive alerts.
5. Show labs to patients as a **horizontal bar with a colored goal range replacing the standard reference range**, plus a plain-language "what this means" label. This matters most for metabolic psychiatry, where the target (therapeutic ketosis) is *outside* standard ranges.
6. Structure the patient dashboard as **three tiers of disclosure**: one glanceable daily view → tap into a 7/30-day trend per marker → raw data for power users (the Whoop/Levels pattern).
7. Make nutrition logging **photo-first and under 10 seconds** — logging friction is the top predictor of tracking abandonment.
8. Price with a **ladder**: accessible entry visit → monthly membership covering messaging + nutritionist sessions + periodic psychiatrist reviews → cheaper "maintenance" tier for stabilized patients (the Brightside/Parsley pattern). Never tie payment to prescription access (the Done indictment lesson).
9. For Brazil: build a **separate, highlighted consent screen** for health data (LGPD Art. 11), a data-rights page, and store the CFM telemedicine consent term with the record. Chronic-care patients need an in-person visit at least every 180 days (CFM 2.314/2022, Art. 6) — the product should track this.
10. Encode the whole visual identity as **shadcn/ui semantic tokens (OKLCH)** so the web dashboard and the future mobile app share one theme.

## 1. Doctor & nutritionist dashboards (provider POV)

**Less is more, and it must be co-designed.** A 2026 JMIR scoping review of clinician dashboards found the successful ones used "targeted, high-resolution displays with limited indicator sets," and that dashboards built with clinician participation reported far stronger usability than purely technical builds — while 78% of projects that skipped structured design guidance struggled with engagement. Practical translation: design the doctor POV *with* your onboarded doctor before the August meeting, and resist adding metrics because they're available. The review's four-pillar checklist — user involvement, actionable content, usability, workflow integration — is a good internal QA rubric. ([JMIR 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e77361))

**The per-patient view: trends beat values.** In a 2024 study of clinical-dashboard components, physicians called score progression over time the most crucial element — "it is very important to look at the progression, not just the individual value" — and rejected charts with many overlaid colored lines. Default to one composite view with toggles for individual measures. A patient-context timeline marking key events (diet started, medication changed, hospitalization) was strongly endorsed; free-text note boxes were not. Physicians also warned against duplicating the EHR — include labs and meds selectively, not exhaustively. ([JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e55267))

**Status flagging without alert fatigue.** Clinicians override 49–96% of interruptive alerts, and excessive alerting is the top documented barrier to clinical decision support actually helping ([JAMIA 2026 review](https://www.metrohealth.org/globalassets/metrohealth-documents/population-health-research-institute/ray-wilson-et-al-2026-alert-fatigue-systematic-review.pdf)). The evidence-backed alternative is passive, in-context status display — a color-coded column on the patient list rather than pop-ups ([passive CDS study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376459605_Addressing_Alert_Fatigue_by_Replacing_a_Burdensome_Interruptive_Alert_with_Passive_Clinical_Decision_Support)). Two design rules follow. First, there is no industry-standard red/amber/green threshold scheme for worklists ([2025 scoping review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1386505625001637)) — you must define your own criteria explicitly (e.g., red = PHQ-9 worsening ≥5 points or ketones below target 7+ days) and document them in the UI. Second, tailor what triggers a flag by role: the doctor and nutritionist should not see identical triggers ([role-tailoring review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31206159/)). One caution echoed by physicians in the 2024 study: real-time crisis alerts (e.g., a suicidality flag at 2 a.m.) create a medico-legal duty to act — surface scores at session time by default and make real-time alerting an explicit clinician opt-in.

**Lab and biomarker trends.** A controlled JAMIA experiment (2,880 assessments) comparing display formats found line graphs were read about twice as fast as tables for longitudinal data; for a patient with many biomarkers, a **sparkline grid** was preferred 16 of 20 times, with an expandable full chart per marker. Never plot different-unit biomarkers on one shared absolute axis (preferred by 0 of 20 participants — it exaggerates trends and hides out-of-range values), always render the shaded reference band because sparklines otherwise hide slightly-out-of-range values, and plot time oldest-to-newest left-to-right — reverse-chronological layouts caused 8 of 20 participants to read a decreasing trend as increasing. ([JAMIA](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/20/2/325/2909219))

**Measurement-based psychiatry is your data engine.** The University of Colorado program that pushed PHQ-9/GAD-7 questionnaires automatically before visits raised completion from 5% to 60–66% — and completion was *higher* in telehealth (70%) than in-person (40%), which favors your model. 78% of clinicians used the scores to guide care. Build automated pre-session check-ins from day one; they populate both the doctor's trend charts and the patient's own progress view. ([Psychiatric Services 2025](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.20240135); [APA implementation guide](https://www.psychiatry.org/getattachment/3d9484a0-4b8e-4234-bd0d-c35843541fce/Resource-Document-on-Implementation-of-Measurement-Based-Care.pdf))

**Schedule view.** Table stakes from the EHRs your doctors will compare you to (SimplePractice, Healthie): a calendar color-coded by appointment type and clinician, automated reminders, and one pane consolidating appointments, messaging, and video links. Healthie's role-based permission model (per-team-member visibility settings) is the reference for your doctor/nutritionist split. ([comparison](https://softwarefinder.com/resources/simplepractice-vs-healthie))

## 2. Patient dashboard (patient POV)

**Three-tier progressive disclosure.** The best-regarded health trackers (Whoop, Oura) structure the experience as: tier 1, a glanceable home answering "how am I doing today?" with one dominant score or priority; tier 2, a tap-through 7/30-day trend per metric; tier 3, raw data for the minority who want it. Each tier is its own screen — expandable accordions on one page add cognitive load rather than reducing it. ([Whoop design breakdown](https://www.925studios.co/blog/whoop-design-breakdown))

**The Levels pattern is your centerpiece.** For a metabolic product, the highest-value patient screen is a shared timeline where ketones, mood, weight, and meals overlay so cause-and-effect is visible at a glance ("I ate X, my ketones did Y, I felt Z"), plus a simple daily report card. ([Levels review](https://www.bettervitals.com/learn/levels-health-review))

**Presenting labs to laypeople — the strongest evidence in this report.** A 2024 systematic review of 18 studies ([JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53993)) is unambiguous: numbers with a reference range are suboptimal. The best-supported format is a horizontal line bar with colored blocks showing where the value sits — and, critically, **personalized goal ranges should replace standard reference ranges**, because substituting goal ranges significantly reduced patient discouragement. For your patients, whose therapeutic ketone targets are "abnormal" by standard ranges, this is not cosmetic — it's the difference between a patient seeing "out of range!" alarm and "on target." Two refinements: add "harm anchors" (a marker labeled "many doctors are not concerned until here") for borderline values, which measurably reduced unnecessary worried calls; and pair every colored display with a plain-language evaluative label, since color alone raised anxiety slightly. Release results immediately — 96% of 8,139 surveyed patients preferred immediate release even for abnormal results — but pair release with explanation, since worry triples for unexplained abnormals. ([OpenNotes](https://www.opennotes.org/open-results/))

**Nutrition logging.** Friction is the dominant retention variable: logging that takes more than ~3 taps or ~10 seconds predicts abandonment. Accept photo, voice, or text, and let AI ask one clarifying follow-up rather than demanding a form (validated in a 2025 field study of multimodal AI meal logging — [SnappyMeal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03907)). Use streaks to reward the logging behavior, not outcomes, and offer streak repair/grace so a missed day doesn't end engagement. ([habit tracker data](https://habi.app/insights/habit-tracker-statistics/))

**The AI summary (your future feature).** The reference implementation is Epic's Emmie in MyChart: an AI plain-language summary appears the moment results land, framed as "what this means" and help *before* the doctor conversation — never as diagnosis. ([Epic](https://www.epic.com/software/ai-patients/)) Trust is the constraint: in one health-system study only 23% of clinicians used AI summaries with patients, and generic-LLM lab interpretation was found superficial and sometimes wrong. Label AI content clearly, show which data points it drew from, and always include a "discuss with your care team" action. ([PMC study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12403210/); [JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53993))

**Appointments that feel like care.** SMS reminders cut no-shows roughly 29–39% ([review](https://www.klara.com/blog/text-message-appointment-reminders-reduce-no-shows-by-38-study-finds)); two-way reminders (confirm/reschedule in one tap) beat notification-only, with optimal cadence around 24 hours + 2 hours before. On the dashboard, the appointment card should show who the session is with (photo, role — doctor vs dietitian), a short prep checklist ("log your ketones this week; questions to ask"), the join link, and one-tap reschedule. Bundling prep with the reminder is what makes it read as care rather than nagging. ([guide](https://appointmentreminder.com/blog/guides/reduce-no-shows/))

## 3. Terminology (English demo)

The market splits cleanly: *appointment* is the scheduling object, *session* is the human encounter, *visit* is billing/medical-records language. Nourish (the leading dietitian telehealth platform) uses exactly this split; Talkiatry uses "visit" because it bills insurance; therapy platforms use "session." Since your platform is a medical model (prescriber + dietitian), the evidence favors "patient": recipients preferred "patient" over "client" in 27 of 33 studies ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6429876/)), and the APA formally endorses it. NHS content research adds two rules: patients don't know the word "clinician" (name the actual role), and it's "dietitian," never "dietician" ([NHS style guide](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/a-to-z-of-nhs-health-writing)). In the US, "nutritionist" is legally unregulated while "registered dietitian" is a protected credential — if your second hire is an RD, say dietitian; if operating under Brazilian credentials ("nutricionista" is a regulated profession in Brazil), "nutritionist" is fine for the Brazilian market but use "dietitian" for any US-facing English.

| Context | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Booking, calendar, reminders | Appointment | Consultation |
| The encounter, warm copy | Session ("your first session") | — |
| Billing/records (psychiatry) | Visit | — |
| People receiving care | Patient (prefer "you/your" in UI) | Client, member, user |
| Staff, generic | Care team / provider | Clinician |
| Staff, specific | Psychiatrist; dietitian (US) / nutritionist (BR) | "Dietician" (misspelling) |
| The plan patients see | Care plan; "program" for a multi-week arc | Protocol, treatment plan (clinical notes only) |

## 4. Visual design: warm + competent

Igor's dark green + cream instinct is validated — it is precisely the palette of the most admired warm-but-clinical brands: One Medical ("premium and human": green/cream, serif+sans pairing, intimate photography — [case study](https://the-brandidentity.com/project/one-medical-moniker)), Parsley Health (calming green on cream, cited as communicating "warmth and clinical credibility" — [examples](https://azurodigital.com/healthcare-website-examples/)), Maven, and Nourish. The risk isn't the palette; it's two failure modes documented by practitioners ([AIGA Eye on Design](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/high-design-healthcare-is-getting-the-millennial-branding-treatment/)): the generic "green leaf + script font" wellness cliché, and over-designed playfulness that alienates patients over 35 (Tia's creative director admitted exactly this). Differentiate with typography and restraint, not more green.

Concrete direction: a warm serif for headings paired with a high-x-height sans for UI and data (the One Medical/Parsley pattern; Headspace's brief for its custom typeface — "flex from playful to clinical" — is the right target); one warm accent (coral/amber) for engagement moments; 16px minimum body text, tabular figures in metric tables, WCAG AA contrast verified *against cream, not white* (cream shrinks contrast headroom). About 8% of men have red-green color blindness — status indicators must always pair color with an icon or label, and red should be reserved for true problems. ([UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2024/07/leveraging-the-psychology-of-color-in-ux-design-for-health-and-wellness-apps.php); [WCAG typography](https://www.a11y-collective.com/blog/wcag-minimum-font-size/))

Implementation: since Lovable outputs React + Tailwind + shadcn/ui, encode everything as shadcn semantic tokens in OKLCH (`--primary` = dark green, `--background` = cream, with defined dark-mode counterparts). A theme generator ([shadcndesign.com](https://www.shadcndesign.com/theme-generator)) turns the brand green into a complete light+dark token set; the same tokens port to React Native/NativeWind for the future app. ([shadcn theming](https://ui.shadcn.com/docs/theming))

## 5. Business model: subscription vs per-session

Verified 2026 market reference points. Telepsychiatry subscriptions: Brightside Health charges $95/month for psychiatry, $299/month for therapy (4 sessions), $349/month combined, and — the clever part — a $45/month "maintenance" tier for stabilized patients (unlimited messaging + monthly check-in, extra visits $50), which retains patients who would otherwise cancel ([Brightside pricing, confirmed on brightside.com](https://www.brightside.com/faqs/how-much-does-it-cost/)). Per-session: Talkspace psychiatry runs $299 initial/$129 follow-up; Grow Therapy $80–250/session. Functional-medicine memberships: Parsley Health ~$150/month membership plus per-visit billing ([Parsley](https://www.parsleyhealth.com/insurance)); Function Health $499/year for 160+ biomarkers tested twice yearly ([Function](https://www.functionhealth.com/pricing)). Dietitian telehealth: Nourish is insurance-first with $145 cash per appointment ([Nourish](https://www.nourish.com/)).

There is no rigorous published A/B evidence that one model "converts best" — claims to that effect are vendor marketing. The observable pattern across the market: low-commitment entry (single paid session) converts acquisition best, while membership economics are what make the business work — hence nearly everyone converges on a ladder: **entry visit → active-care membership → maintenance tier**. For Metabolicamente, a natural shape is: paid initial psychiatric evaluation, then a monthly membership bundling nutritionist sessions, messaging, and periodic psychiatrist reviews, with a maintenance tier after stabilization. Retention context: episodic telehealth retains ~30% of patients; subscription telehealth churn benchmarks run 5–8% monthly with month 3 the peak churn point — design the month-2-to-4 experience (visible progress, human touchpoints) deliberately. (Churn figures are directional, from industry benchmark aggregators, not peer-reviewed.)

One bright-line rule: the Done Health founders were criminally convicted in 2024–25 over a subscription model the DOJ framed as fee-for-prescription-access. Membership must buy access, coordination, and support — never a guaranteed prescription. ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/digital-health-company-and-medical-practice-indicted-100m-adderall-distribution-scheme))

## 6. Compliance (affects UX, so plan it now)

**Brazil / LGPD.** Health data is "dado pessoal sensível": processing needs specific, *highlighted* consent for a defined purpose (LGPD Art. 11) — a separate consent screen in onboarding, not a line in the ToS — plus product support for data-subject rights (access, correction, deletion, portability; Art. 18). Fines reach 2% of revenue up to R$50M. A DPO ("encarregado") exemption exists for small processors (ANPD Res. 2/2022, Art. 11) but does *not* apply to high-risk processing, and whether a health platform qualifies needs legal review — appointing one is the safe default. (Verified against the [official resolution](https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/acesso-a-informacao/institucional/atos-normativos/regulamentacoes_anpd/resolucao-cd-anpd-no-2-de-27-de-janeiro-de-2022).)

**Brazilian telemedicine (CFM 2.314/2022, verified in the official text).** First consultations may be fully virtual, but chronic/long-term care requires an in-person visit at intervals **not exceeding 180 days** (Art. 6) — build a "next in-person visit due" tracker into the doctor POV. The patient's telehealth consent term must be stored with the electronic record. ([official PDF](https://sistemas.cfm.org.br/normas/arquivos/resolucoes/BR/2022/2314_2022.pdf)) Note: the nutritionist side is governed by CFN (not CFM) resolutions — a follow-up item.

**US / HIPAA (only when real US patients exist).** Supabase — Lovable's native backend — supports HIPAA via a BAA, but only on the Team plan ($599/month) plus a separately priced HIPAA add-on, with obligations on your side (MFA, point-in-time recovery, RLS, PHI segregation). Confirmed on [supabase.com/pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing) and [HIPAA docs](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/security/hipaa-compliance). A demo with fake data triggers none of this — defer until real PHI exists.

**English demo (now → early August).** Fake data, no compliance burden. Priorities: the three POVs with the patterns above — doctor POV with patient list + status column + per-patient sparkline grid and event timeline; patient POV with the three-tier home, one Levels-style overlay chart, goal-range lab bars, and an appointment card with prep checklist; nutritionist POV as a role-filtered variant of the doctor view. Use the terminology table throughout. This is a Lovable iteration job — no code export needed yet.

**November MVP.** Real auth (three roles), Supabase backend, pre-session PHQ-9/GAD-7 check-ins, SMS reminders (24h + 2h, two-way), LGPD consent screen + data-rights page, CFM consent term storage, the 180-day in-person tracker, and the pricing ladder with a maintenance tier. Build in Claude Code on the exported repo. Defer: AI summary (do it right — labeled, source-linked — rather than early), HIPAA/US market, native mobile app (the shadcn token architecture keeps that door open).
